<h2>About the family</h2>
<p>The family Termitidae was established by Latreille in 1802, with the type genus Termes Linnaeus, 1758. The family name is formed from the type genus stem Termit- plus the family-level suffix -idae. This checklist records 280 genera and 2144 species.</p>
<p>This family has a pantropical distribution, with representatives in all major zoogeographic regions of the tropics and subtropics, and a few species extending into warm-temperate zones. Its feeding habits are the most diverse among termite families, encompassing wood-feeding, grass-feeding, leaf-litter-feeding, humus-feeding and fungus-cultivating types; a few species feed on lichens and algae growing on bark (such as the genus Constrictotermes), while inquilinous species feed on the nest material and feces of their host nest (such as the genus Inquilinitermes); about sixty percent of species are specialized soil-humus feeders. Unlike other families, the hindgut of this family is highly compartmentalized, and its digestive symbionts are dominated by bacteria and archaea. The most conspicuous shared feature of termitid soldiers is the unpaired, sac-like frontal gland (35), whose external opening is the fontanelle, yet defensive strategies diverge along this structure. The Nasutitermitinae is the largest subfamily of the family; in it the front of the soldier's head is prolonged into a conical nasus, the mandibles are reduced, and the soldier relies entirely on the frontal gland to eject a sticky secretion through the fontanelle at the tip of the nasus to entangle enemies; the Syntermitinae, which also possess a nasus, retain well-developed mandibles and employ both mechanical and chemical defense. The other extreme is seen in the snapping-mandible groups of the Mirocapritermitinae, such as the genus Pericapritermes, which repel enemies by a sudden crosswise strike of the two mandibles, while the frontal gland is poorly developed; Globitermes sulphureus goes even further, using rupture of the frontal gland for suicidal defense. A few lineages of the family have lost the soldier caste, the Neotropical species of the Apicotermitinae being one example, in which defense is undertaken entirely by the workers. In the alates the pronotum is saddle-shaped, the hind wing lacks an anal lobe and has simplified venation, and a fontanelle and ocelli are present; in the workers the postclypeus is swollen into a globular form and the hindgut has a mixed segment, and the antennae usually have 13-15 segments, while those of the soldiers usually have 12-16 segments. The detailed diagnostic characters of the soldiers and workers of each subfamily are given in the following subfamily sections. Nesting patterns are diverse, including subterranean nests, surface mounds and arboreal carton nests, and the nests of this family are the most conspicuous ecological-engineering structures of tropical forests and savannas. The Termitidae is the most species-rich group within the Neoisoptera; it began to radiate around the end of the Eocene and underwent a rapid internal radiation during the Paleogene.</p>
<p>The family is currently divided into 18 subfamilies (21): Amitermitinae (14 genera, 194 species), Apicotermitinae (68 genera, 241 species), Crepititermitinae (1 genera, 1 species), Cubitermitinae (28 genera, 149 species), Cylindrotermitinae (2 genera, 9 species), Engelitermitinae (1 genera, 1 species), Foraminitermitinae (3 genera, 10 species), Forficulitermitinae (1 genera, 1 species), Macrotermitinae (12 genera, 373 species), Microcerotermitinae (2 genera, 150 species), Mirocapritermitinae (14 genera, 135 species), Nasutitermitinae (81 genera, 606 species), Neocapritermitinae (3 genera, 20 species), Promirotermitinae (2 genera, 39 species), Protohamitermitinae (2 genera, 2 species), Sphaerotermitinae (1 genera, 1 species), Syntermitinae (22 genera, 105 species), and Termitinae (23 genera, 107 species). The economic damage caused by this family is detailed under each subfamily.</p>
